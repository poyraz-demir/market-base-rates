Should you wait for a crash? The base rates, reproduced from primary data

Distributions and counts for the US stock market, computed with plain pandas from Robert Shiller’s monthly data, Aswath Damodaran’s annual returns, FRED and Yahoo Finance daily series. One command (python run_all.py) regenerates every table; this page is rendered from those CSV files and contains no number typed in by hand. There are no forecasts and no recommendations here. Each section answers one question people ask before investing and then says what the table does and does not show.

Daily series end 2026-09-18; Shiller monthly data through 2026-09 (September price = 1 September close, recent CPI months estimated by Shiller); Damodaran annual data through 2025; FRED CPI through 2026-08. Generated 2026-09-21 by python run_all.py.

What happened after CAPE was this high?

Shiller’s cyclically adjusted price/earnings ratio for 2026-09 is 40.58, against a median of 16.61 across the 1,749 months with a reading. Only 20 of those months were higher; the peak was 44.20 in 1999-12.

The table below takes every month in a bucket and looks 1, 3, 5 and 10 years ahead, in real (CPI-deflated) total-return terms.

What this does and does not show

The windows overlap. Consecutive monthly starts share almost their whole path, so n counts windows, not independent outcomes. The next table lists the contiguous runs behind the 10-year rows: CAPE > 30 rests on 4 runs, CAPE > 35 on 2 runs inside the dot-com bubble, and CAPE > 40 is a single episode (1999-01 to 2000-09, 21 months) counted 21 times. The 5-month run that began in 2026-05 has no outcome yet, at any horizon.

Two regimes sit in one bucket. 58% of CAPE > 30 months (every entry from mid-2017 on) do not have a 10-year outcome, and the entries that do come from 1929 & 1997-2002. Splitting the bucket by regime shows that the pooled median describes neither group: the 1929 & 1997-2002 entries were followed by a negative median real return at 3 and 5 years, the 2017-2021 entries by a positive return in every 3- and 5-year window.

What does missing the best days cost, and where are those days?

Three daily series, each over its own full span: the S&P 500 price index from 1927-12-30 (no dividends), the S&P 500 total return index from 1988-01-04, and the SPY ETF from 1993-01-29 (dividends reinvested, net of the fund’s expenses). Each scenario deletes the listed days and compounds the rest.

What this does and does not show

Removing the best days and removing the worst days are the same arithmetic, and both are shown because neither is something an investor can actually do: nobody is out of the market only on the best days. The reason the cost looks so large is where those days sit. Of the 50 best days, 28–47 (depending on the series) came while the index was more than 20% below its high, and 27–31 fell within five trading days of one of the 50 worst days. Big up days are part of crashes, not a reward for sitting through calm markets.

What happened to people who waited in cash?

Five start dates: one chosen for what was said that day (Greenspan 'irrational exuberance' speech, 1996-12-05) and four first-trading-days of a January. “Cash” is a rolling position in 3-month Treasury bills; “stocks” is the S&P 500 with dividends reinvested. Both paths run to the data end.

The last table turns waiting into a rule: stay in T-bills until the index has fallen 10, 20 or 30% from its highest close since the start date, then buy everything.

What this does and does not show

These are five paths, not a distribution, and the cash series only exists from late 1981, so no earlier start is possible. From 1996-12-05 the cash position was ahead on 3.52% of trading days and for the last time on 2009-07-14; the largest share in any case is 15.38% (start 2025-01-02). The dip rule can win: the 2018 start ends +16.83% ahead by waiting for a 30% fall, because that fall came on 2020-03-20. The 2021 start shows the other side: a 30% fall never came, and the money is still in T-bills, −46.08% against investing at once.

How often do drawdowns come, and how long does recovery take?

Price index, daily closes, from 1927-12-30. An episode starts at a running high, ends when the old high is regained, and counts if the decline in between reached the threshold.

A decline of 10% or more has arrived once every 3.8 years on average, one of 20% or more once every 8.2 years. The median 20% episode took 481 days from peak to trough and 532 more to get back to the old high.

What this does and does not show

Everything here is nominal and price-only, because a daily total-return series only exists from 1988. The 25.0-year recovery from the 1929 peak is therefore overstated: with dividends reinvested and the deflation of the 1930s, the real recovery came earlier. 4 of the 12 declines of 20% or more began after the total-return series starts. Intra-year drawdowns are the base rate for “something will go wrong this year”: in 62.2% of years the index fell 10% or more at some point, and 66 of 98 years still closed higher than they opened.

Buying at an all-time high vs any day

Over the last 20 years the price index closed at a new all-time high on 9.5% of trading days and within 5% of one on 52.3% and within 10% on 67.2%. A rule of not buying near a high excludes most days.

The next tables take every trading day as a purchase date, group the days by distance from the running high, and compare the return over the following year.

What this does and does not show

In the total-return series a purchase on a new-high day (n = 764) was followed by a median 1-year return of 15.85%, against 14.38% for a random day, and was negative 14.40% of the time against 16.81%. The distribution after a high is narrower, not higher: the 5th percentile is −9.75% against −18.73%. n counts overlapping daily windows, and the total-return series covers 1988 onwards only, a period with 4 declines of 20% or more; the price-index table reaches back to 1928 and includes 1929, which is why its worst cases are so much deeper.

What does waiting cost today? The real yield on cash

Real yield here is the 3-month Treasury bill yield minus CPI inflation over the previous twelve months. On 2026-09-17 that is 4.12% minus 3.35%, a real yield of 0.74%. The 2021 average was −4.40%.

What this does and does not show

Waiting in cash forgoes whatever stocks return; that cost is in the sections above and does not depend on the rate. What the rate changes is whether cash also loses purchasing power while you wait. Between 2009 and 2021 the real yield was negative in 83.33% of months (mean −1.27%); since 2023 in 11.63% of months (mean 1.38%). The whole 1982–2026-08 history averages 0.80%. The series starts in 1982 because FRED’s daily 3-month yield does, and the latest CPI print lags the yield by a few weeks.

Lump sum vs dollar-cost averaging

Each of the 453 monthly start dates since 1988 is a window. “Lump sum” invests everything on day one; “DCA” invests in equal monthly slices over 3, 6 or 12 months, with the uninvested part either earning the 3-month T-bill rate or nothing. Both are measured after 12 months.

What this does and does not show

Spreading the purchase over 12 months lost to investing at once in 76.6% of windows, by a median 4.9% of the amount invested, when the idle cash earned T-bills. What it bought was a better bad case: the 5th-percentile outcome is 0.905x for DCA against 0.815x for the lump sum. Either choice beat staying in cash in 74.4%–80.1% of windows. The design follows Vanguard’s 2023 study but uses a different index and period, so the hit rates are comparable in spirit, not identical.

Data and method

All computation is plain pandas in eight scripts, one per question; python run_all.py writes results/*.csv and results/SUMMARY.md, and python docs/build_site.py renders this page from those files. Every daily series is cut at DATA_END = 2026-09-18 so a later download reproduces the same tables. Tables generated 2026-09-21.

Robert Shiller, Irrational Exuberance data (shillerdata.com)
Monthly S&P composite price, dividends, earnings, CPI and CAPE from 1871. Snapshot downloaded 2026-09-20. Used for the CAPE tables.
Aswath Damodaran, Historical Returns on Stocks, Bonds and Bills (NYU Stern)
Annual returns from 1928. Snapshot downloaded 2026-09-19. Used for long-run returns.
FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis
DGS3MO (3-month Treasury yield), CPIAUCSL and CPIAUCNS (CPI-U) and others. Downloaded 2026-09-19. Used for cash returns, real yields and inflation adjustment.
Yahoo Finance daily series
^GSPC (price, 1927 onwards), ^SP500TR (total return, 1988 onwards), SPY (adjusted close, 1993 onwards). Downloaded 2026-09-20, last observation 2026-09-18. Not redistributed; python fetch.py re-downloads them.

Limitations

  • Overlapping windows. All forward-return tables (scripts 02, 06, 08) use monthly or daily start dates, so consecutive windows share almost their whole path. n counts windows, not independent observations. Script 02 lists the contiguous runs behind each CAPE bucket: the CAPE > 30 ten-year statistics come from four runs, the CAPE > 35 ones from two runs inside the dot-com bubble, and CAPE > 40 is a single episode (1999-2000) counted 21 times. Non-overlapping variants (e.g. January entries only) leave too few observations to describe a distribution, which is why the overlapping tables are shown together with the episode count.
  • Two regimes, one bucket. CAPE > 30 entries in 1929 and 1997-2002 were followed by negative real returns; CAPE > 30 entries in 2017-2021 were followed by strongly positive ones. The pooled median describes neither. 58 % of CAPE > 30 months (entries from mid-2017 on) do not have a ten-year outcome yet.
  • Daily total return only from 1988. Scripts 03, 05 and 06 use the price-only ^GSPC series before 1988 (no dividends), which understates absolute returns by roughly 3-4 percentage points a year but barely affects comparisons within one series. The 1929-1954 "25-year recovery" is nominal, price-only; with dividends reinvested and 1930s deflation the real recovery came earlier.
  • Cash series only from September 1981 (start of FRED DGS3MO), so the real-cash-yield history (07) starts in 1982 and the waiting-in-cash cases (04) cannot go back further. TB3MS/DTB3 were not obtained.
  • Missing best days vs missing worst days are symmetric calculations; the asymmetry people quote exists because best and worst days cluster in the same crashes (27-31 of the 50 best days sit within 5 trading days of one of the 50 worst, depending on the series).
  • No taxes, no fees, no slippage anywhere except the SPY series (which is net of the fund's expense ratio).
  • Recent CPI in Shiller's file is estimated by the author for the last few months; the September price is the 1 September close. FRED CPI runs through August 2026.
  • Lump sum vs DCA (08) follows the design of Vanguard's 2023 paper but on a different index and period (S&P 500 total return from 1988 vs Russell 3000 and MSCI World from 1976-79), so hit ratios are comparable in spirit, not identical.
  • Not obtained, so not reproduced here: Vanguard 2012 "Dollar-Cost Averaging Just Means Taking Risk Later" (links dead), DALBAR QAIB and Morningstar "Mind the Gap" (paywalled / blocked), Hartford Funds / Ned Davis "missing the best days" tables (403), S&P Dow Jones Indices' own 100-year total-return series (403).
